01 1956—1987

Period one

Dissident Activity Under Soviet Rule

Long before entering government, Gamsakhurdia participated in underground publishing, human-rights advocacy and campaigns for Georgia's cultural, religious and political freedoms. Arrest, surveillance and restrictions became part of this political life.

1956

Early Anti-Soviet Activity

As a school student, he joined a circle of young activists distributing proclamations against Soviet intervention in Hungary, leading to an early arrest.

1974—1976

Human-Rights Organization

Together with Merab Kostava and other activists, he helped organize human-rights work and the Georgian Helsinki movement, documenting violations of freedoms promised under international agreements.

1977

Arrest and Prosecution

Soviet authorities arrested Gamsakhurdia and Kostava in a wider campaign against dissident and samizdat activity. The case became one of the defining episodes of Georgian political dissent.

1979—1987

Return to Activism

Following release, he returned to literary, religious and political activity, continued underground publication, and campaigned around political prisoners and national rights.

02 1987—1991

Period two

The National Independence Movement

In the final years of the Soviet Union, dissident networks moved into public politics. Demonstrations, political organizations, elections and a national referendum transformed demands for rights into a program for restored state independence.

1987—1989

Mass Politics and Public Mobilization

Political activity moved from underground circles to public organizations and demonstrations. Gamsakhurdia and Merab Kostava emerged as central figures in the national movement.

9 April 1989

The Tbilisi Tragedy

Soviet troops violently dispersed a peaceful demonstration in Tbilisi. The deaths and injuries intensified public demands for separation from Soviet rule.

October 1990

Victory in Multiparty Elections

The Round Table—Free Georgia coalition won the elections to the Supreme Council, placing the independence movement in government and Gamsakhurdia at the head of the republic's political authority.

31 March 1991

Referendum on Independence

Voters were asked whether Georgia's independence should be restored on the basis of the Act of 26 May 1918. The referendum produced an overwhelming affirmative result.

9 April 1991

Restoration of State Independence

The Supreme Council adopted the Act restoring Georgia's state independence, formally connecting the new republic to the democratic state established in 1918.

03 1991—1993

Period three

Presidency, Armed Overthrow and Civil War

Independence created a state but not yet stable institutions. The presidency unfolded amid economic collapse, internal opposition, disputes over political authority, armed formations and regional conflict. Confrontation in Tbilisi ended in the government's violent overthrow and was followed by exile, renewed fighting and civil war.

26 May 1991

Election as President

Gamsakhurdia won the nationwide presidential election and became the first president of the restored Republic of Georgia.

1991

A State Under Immediate Pressure

The new government faced the construction of institutions, economic crisis, Soviet and Russian pressure, regional disputes, and a rapidly escalating confrontation with domestic opponents.

December 1991—January 1992

Armed Overthrow

Fighting in central Tbilisi between government forces and armed opposition groups ended with the capture of government buildings and Gamsakhurdia's departure from Georgia.

1992—1993

Exile, Return and Civil War

Supporters of the deposed government resisted the new authorities. Gamsakhurdia returned to western Georgia in 1993, and the struggle became part of the wider civil-war crisis confronting the country.
